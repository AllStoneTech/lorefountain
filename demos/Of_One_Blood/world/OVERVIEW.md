## Premise

[[Reuel Briggs]], a brilliant, friendless Harvard medical student, conceals two things: a private gift for mesmerism, and an ancestry he has always half-suspected and hidden deliberately. Both concealments — see [[The Hidden Self]] and [[Racial Passing]] — drive a story that takes him from a Boston hospital ward to the hidden African city of [[Telassar]], and reveals that three people who don't know they're related share the same buried royal bloodline.

## Setting

Turn-of-the-century Boston high society and the Vance family estate outside it; the [[Charles River]], where a canoe accident and a drowning change everything; and, in the second half of the story, an expedition to [[Meroe]] whose pyramids hide the passage to [[Telassar]] — a hidden, learned and advanced African city, ruled by [[Queen Candace]] and awaiting the return of an heir of the ancient king Ergamenes.

## Key Characters

- **[[Reuel Briggs]]** — a Harvard medical student passing as white; a mesmerist; ultimately recognized as the heir of the [[House of Telassar]] and crowned King Ergamenes.
- **[[Dianthe Lusk]]** — a jubilee soloist Reuel revives after a train wreck leaves her presumed dead and memoryless; marries Reuel the morning he sails, is later coerced into marrying Aubrey, and dies after Aubrey forces her to drink the poison she'd prepared for him.
- **[[Aubrey Livingston]]** — Reuel's closest friend and secret rival, engaged to Molly Vance while obsessed with Dianthe; in truth Reuel and Dianthe's own brother (Hannah says "own blood brother," Jim Titus says "half"), and the story's antagonist.
- **[[Hannah]]** — grandmother to all three, who swapped Aubrey for the Livingstons' dead infant and eventually reveals the truth of their shared parentage to Dianthe.

## Synopsis

Reuel Briggs revives Dianthe Lusk after a train wreck leaves her presumed dead, falls in love with her, and marries her the morning he leaves for an expedition to Meroe — a post arranged by his friend Aubrey Livingston, who has quietly known Reuel's ancestry since they met. In Reuel's absence, a canoe accident drowns Aubrey's fiancée Molly Vance and leaves Dianthe presumed dead as well, though Aubrey has actually pulled her from the water and hidden her away; he tells her Reuel is dead and coerces her into marrying him. In Africa, a vision in the desert shows Reuel that his letters are being withheld by Aubrey's agent Jim Titus; when the mail brings news of Dianthe's "death," grief drives him into the pyramid, where he is taken into Telassar and crowned King Ergamenes — the lotus-lily birthmark and his mother, Mira, tying him to the royal line. Jim, dying in a serpent pit, tells him Aubrey plotted it all and that Dianthe is his own sister. Meanwhile, back in America, intercepted mail exposes Aubrey's fraud to Dianthe, and Hannah reveals that Reuel, Dianthe, and Aubrey are all Mira's children. Dianthe tries to poison Aubrey herself; he forces her to drink it instead, and she dies the night Reuel finally makes it home. Aubrey, left to his lawyers, is entranced by Ai and ordered to die "by thine own hand" under ancient Telassar law; he is found drowned in the same stretch of the Charles River that took Molly. Reuel returns to Telassar to rule beside Queen Candace, uneasy to the end about how much of the outside world is closing in on his people's hiding place.

## Themes

The novel's title and central argument, spoken by Reuel himself in its opening chapter, is that "the hidden self" lies quiescent in everyone — see [[The Hidden Self]]. Reuel hides his ancestry and his mesmeric gift; Dianthe loses access to her own identity entirely; Aubrey hides his crimes behind a friend's face, and his own hidden self — Mira's blood, swapped into the Livingston line at birth — is the deepest concealment in the book (the text never shows whether he learns it). [[Racial Passing]] runs through all three as the same wound in different forms: chosen and costly for Reuel, unknowing for Dianthe once her memory is gone, and, as far as the text shows, unsuspected for Aubrey, who was raised as the Livingstons' white heir. [[Ethiopian Primacy]] gives the hidden city of Telassar its historical weight — not a fantasy, but, in the novel's own argument, the last living proof of a real, buried African history.
