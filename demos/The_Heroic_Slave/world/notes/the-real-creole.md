# The real Creole (historical context — not from the novella)

Everything here comes from outside Douglass's text. The points marked **checked** were verified against the published sources listed at the bottom (a web search, November 2026); the points marked **unchecked** are recalled from the general historical record and should be checked before relying on them. None of it is used as fact in the scripts except where a script line says so. Create entities from it only as `canon_status: tentative`.

## Checked

- **Douglass's story is based on a real event.** On 7 November 1841 enslaved people aboard the brig *Creole*, bound from Virginia to New Orleans in the coastwise slave trade, rose, took the ship and sailed to Nassau in the Bahamas. A man named Madison Washington is recorded as their leader. The *Creole* reached Nassau on 9 November 1841.
- **Departure port.** The real ship sailed from **Hampton Roads, Virginia**, not Richmond. (The novella has the brig weigh anchor in the stream at Richmond.)
- **The rising and the dead.** Eighteen enslaved men attacked the crew with Washington (nineteen in all, the same number as the novella). They killed one of the slave traders aboard, **John R. Hewell**, and wounded the ship's captain, **Robert Ensor**. In the novella the captain (Clarke) and the owner (Jameson) are both killed; those names and that outcome are Douglass's, not the record's.
- **Numbers.** Sources give about 135 enslaved people aboard (the novella says 130). The revolt is usually described as freeing about 128 people.
- **Aftermath.** The nineteen were detained in Nassau. On 16 April 1842 the Vice-Admiralty court released the mutineers, including Washington. (The novella ends at the wharf, with no mention of a later hearing.)

## Unchecked

- **Why Nassau mattered.** Britain had abolished slavery across its empire in the 1830s, which is why the authorities would not treat the captives as property. In the novella the soldiers say they "do not recognize persons as property". The affair became a diplomatic dispute between the United States and Britain and is remembered as one of the most successful slave revolts at sea.
- **The first mate.** The real mate's name is not given in the sources checked; the novella's Tom Grant is Douglass's character.
- **Douglass's choice.** He published *The Heroic Slave* in 1853, some years after the event, as an argument that the Founders' principles belong to Black Americans too. His Madison, unlike the historical record, speaks like a statesman.
- **The "dismal swamp".** The novella's "dismal swamps" are plural and lower-case; the real Great Dismal Swamp of Virginia and North Carolina did shelter people who had escaped slavery, but the text never says it is that one.
- **The soldiers.** Whether the "black soldiers" of Nassau were from a West India regiment is an inference, not in the text.
