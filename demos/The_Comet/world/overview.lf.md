# The Comet

> **Content note.** This adaptation preserves the period language of W.E.B. Du Bois's 1920 story, including a racial slur, and uses period terms such as "Negro" and "colored." It does so because it adapts a historical text faithfully; the words are not softened or removed.

## Premise

The messenger on the bank's steps is "outside the world." The comet takes the city out of it, and for a few hours the only two people either can find alive are a Black workingman and a white heiress who yesterday would never have looked at each other. Then the world comes back and puts them in their places.

## Setting

New York City, on the day a new comet's tail sweeps past at noon, from the bank on Broadway and its flooded vaults, through Wall Street, Fifth Avenue, Madison Square, Harlem, the telephone exchange, the cable office, and the roof of the Metropolitan Tower. The text gives no year; its cars, telephones and skyscraper mark it as the 1910s-1920s.

## Key Characters

- **[[Jim Davis]]**: bank messenger, sent into the lower vaults because it is too dangerous for "more valuable men."
- **[[Julia]]**: a young woman of wealth, developing photographs of the comet when it passes.
- **[[Julia's Father]]** and **[[Fred]]**: the white-haired man and the younger man who arrive when the world returns.
- **[[Jim's Wife]]**: the toil-worn woman who finds him at the end.
- **[[The Narrator]]**: an adaptation device, not in the source.

## Synopsis

Six episodes. 1x01: a morning of comet talk on Broadway; Jim goes down into the vaults, finds a hidden room with an iron chest of gold, and is sealed in. 1x02: he emerges to a silent bank, silent Wall Street, a silent city, and drives up Fifth Avenue to a living voice. 1x03: Julia, her dead household, Harlem, and her father's unsent note. 1x04: the telephone exchange, the cable office, and the water. 1x05: the roof, the rockets, the comet, and two visions. 1x06: the honking motor, the returning world, and what it does to what they saw.

## Themes

How fast human distinctions come back; what a society needs the poor and the Black to be for it; the intimacy of shared catastrophe and its brief half-life; the distance between a woman's vision of "primal woman" and the line "I've always liked your people."
