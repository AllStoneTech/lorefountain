# Source material

> **CONTENT NOTE.** The novel was serialized in 1902-03 and published as a book in 1902. It depicts slavery, whipping, a near-lynching and mob violence, and its characters use period racial slurs. This adaptation keeps the language in the mouths of the characters who use it and never in a caption. See the README's content note.

## The text

- **Work:** *Winona: A Tale of Negro Life in the South and Southwest*, by Pauline E. Hopkins (1859–1930).
- **Edition:** Project Gutenberg eBook #70087 (<https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/70087>), transcribed by Steve Mattern and many proofreading and formatting volunteers, released February 20, 2023. Gutenberg gives the original publication as The Colored Co-operative Publishing Company, 1902. The serialization dates (1902-03) are from the project brief; the Gutenberg text itself carries no such statement.
- **Public domain:** worldwide (published 1902; the author died in 1930). The Gutenberg license section is kept verbatim at the end of `winona.md`.
- **Full text:** [`winona.md`](winona.md), seventeen chapters, 44,007 words in the body. See `README.md` in this folder for how it was converted from Gutenberg's plain text (paragraphs reflowed, headings and a contents list added, italic markers turned into Markdown emphasis, nothing in the text changed). It is **not** edited by any script in this project.

## What was taken

- **The plot, in order,** every chapter, with the novel's dates where it gives them ("a June day", "a sultry evening in July", "early in the spring of 1856", "the 26th day of May next").
- **The dialogue, where it can be lettered.** Lines are the novel's own wording, in its dialect (Maybee's, Tennie's, Aunt Vinnie's, the Rangers'), cut to fit the lettering budget (25 words a balloon, 40 a panel) by splitting a long speech into consecutive balloons or by dropping clauses. The set-piece speeches (the Colonel's 1854 St. Joseph speech, Steward's defense of fighting, Warren's argument on the peak, Thomson's confession) are given close to whole. A script check (every lettered balloon and caption sentence of three words or more, compared with the Gutenberg text after lower-casing and stripping punctuation) found **793 of 929 sentences identical to a sentence in the novel**. The other 136 are light edits and the invented lines listed below; a few of those are false alarms where the check split a sentence at "Mr." or "Capt.".
- **The narrator's sentences as captions,** quoted from the text, never invented, and never carrying a slur.
- **Period language, including slurs,** in the characters' mouths as the novel has it.
- **The structure of the mystery:** Pendleton's account of the Carlingford case in Issue 1, the Colonel's slips in Issue 3, and the full confession in Issue 8, so the reader gets the clues in the order the book gives them.

## What was changed or left out

- **Left out:** the narrator's essays (on social equality in Chapter XI, on religion and the "arm of restraint" in Chapter XII, on mobs, on Gov. Davis and the South's "reversion" in Chapter IV); the long scene-setting passages of Chapters I and IX; Maybee's anecdotes about 'Tarius's baptism (Chapter XIV) and about his Lawrence claim and the "transfiguration of souls" (Chapter VII); most of the Kansas Free-State politics in Maybee's and Thomson's speeches; Warren's reflection on Christ at the stake (kept as a single caption); the description of what is done to a man in the lower cell, which the novel itself says "must not be described".
- **Compressed:** the sack of Osawatamie and Bud Wilson's wife's death (told in a line and not shown); the Maybee wharf conversation; the three-day wagon journey after the jail break.
- **Re-staged:** every place where the novel tells and the comic must show. Where the novel narrates an event the script supplies the picture (flashbacks for White Eagle's past, for Pendleton's account, and for Thomson's confession), but it does not add facts.
- **Direct speech from narration:** the Colonel's testimony at the trial (the novel summarizes it); Brown describing his insurrection plan; Warren promising money and arms; Winona's prayer "Help me to help save him!" (the novel says she prayed it); Judah's "The white man has the advantage in all things" on the morning ride and his "Why should I hesitate?" in the woods (both are his thoughts in the novel, spoken aloud here); Warren's "You shall not be friendless if I can help you" (his thought, spoken to Winona).
- **Names added to speakers the novel leaves unnamed:** "Gatekeeper" (the man at the bell gate), "Jailer's Man", "Whipper", "Visitor" (the man with the carpet bag), "Messenger", "Ranger".
- **The leader of the hunters in Issue 1** is not named in the dialogue; the panel direction identifies him to the artist as Colonel Titus, because the novel says in Chapter XV that Titus and Thomson were "the strangers" who came to the island.

## Invented lines, sound effects and images

The script invents no plot fact. It does invent the following; every one is a lettering or staging choice.

- **Sound effects and lettering in every issue:** BOOOM, KRAK, HOOOOOO (the horn), KRAKOOOM, THWAP, WHA-HOOOEY, SHHH-WAP, clop-clop-clop, SKRRRRRRK, SPLASH, TAA-RAA-RAAA, YAAAAAH and the like. The novel supplies the events and sometimes the sounds ("a deafening crash", "the clink of metal against stone"); the spelling is the comic's.
- **Ages and looks not in the text:** Judah is drawn "about sixteen" in Issue 1; Winona's age in Issue 1 follows Chapter II ("not more than fourteen") and is not stated on the pages.
- **Speaker attributions the text leaves open:** Chapter I's "Likely nigger" is given to Thomson (the leader answers); Chapter XVI's first speech to the waking Maybee ("you know we won the fight... Captain Brown is a shootin' all the pris'ners") is given to Steward, and the joke "ain't that tough fer a prefesser?" to Maybee; the novel's wording leaves it unclear who says which.
- **Relabelling of the two Lord Georges:** in Pendleton's speech (Issue 1) the dying baronet is "Old Lord George". In Warren's line in Issue 3 ("Lord George's younger son, Henry") the novel's wording is kept as it is; see the note *which-lord-george*.
- **Omitted line:** Warren's "Your uncle died six months ago" (Chapter VI), which cannot be squared with Chapter XVII's living "old Lord George".
- **Invented staging:** a trunk with nothing in it at White Eagle's cottage (Issue 2); a drill ground, a ledger and a piano lesson standing for the novel's summary of Magnolia Farm; the lamplit flashback frame for Pendleton's story; the dying man's confession flashbacks (Titus crouched at a bend in the shore, the valet and his sister, the London doorway) and the flashback of White Eagle handing Maybee the tin box; a boy's shoe beside the well where Allen Pinks vanishes; the Montreal ship and the knighting hall in the epilogue; the lamp lit in the hotel window on the last pages.
- **Light edits to the novel's wording** (the large majority of the 136 non-identical sentences): the narrator's third-person sentences recast as Warren's first-person captions in Issue 1; the narrator's captions trimmed (for example the opening, "In the early 50's, small bands of Indians were still familiar figures on both borders"); indirect speech made direct (Warren to the jailer, "Do you call yourself a man to so insult a wounded stranger?"); Thomson's confession split into voice-over balloons laid over flashback panels. Maybee's "You want to go home?" (Issue 6), Steward's "Go up, child, and I'll take the ladder away behind you" (Issue 4), Warren's "I will take you to England" at the grave (Issue 2), the Messenger's "Thomson is conscious, and going fast. He wants the Parson and Winona." (Issue 7) and the Judge's sentence (trimmed) are the nearest the script comes to new lines; each is a plain rendering of something the narration says happened.
- **A choice the novel does not state:** after the jail break Thomson is shown locked in the cell with the officials (Issue 6, page 8). The novel says only that "the officials" and "the prisoners" are locked in; see the note *what-happens-to-thomson-after-the-jail*.

## Known gaps and contradictions in the source

- **Two Lord Georges** (Chapters II, VI, XVI, XVII); see *which-lord-george*.
- **Dating:** "the early 50's" and "the new act... jes' passed" in Chapter III against "the spring of 1856" and "two years" of captivity in Chapter IV; a hanging set for a Friday, May 26th, which falls on a Friday in 1854 and 1865 but a Monday in 1856; see *dating-the-erie-chapters*.
- **Distance to the island:** "about a mile" from Grand Island (Chapter I), "five miles between the mainland and the island" (Chapter II).
- **Thomson's escape** after Chapter XIII; **Gideon Holmes's** fate after Chapter XV; **Nokomis's** fate after Chapter III; **Colonel Titus's death:** the novel shows Judah pursuing him and the Colonel falling and later says "Devil's got the colonel"; that Judah killed him is the natural reading and is how the script draws it.
- **The novel's own name for the nephew:** the old lord's nephew is never given the surname Carlingford; he is Colonel Titus throughout, and the novel does not explain whether Titus is an alias.

## Verification status

The novel was read in full from `winona.md` before any file was written, and the plot, chapter order, names and dialogue were checked against it while writing; the sentence check above is the only mechanical test, and it covers lettering only. Art direction (panel descriptions) was not checked line by line: it draws on the novel's physical descriptions and stages its events, and anything beyond that is listed above as invented staging. The seven "open questions" in `world/notes/` list what the source leaves unsettled. The historical background (John Brown's camp, Osawatamie, the Fugitive Slave Act) is only what the novel says; nothing was imported from outside it, and nothing in this folder confirms the novel's own historical claims.
